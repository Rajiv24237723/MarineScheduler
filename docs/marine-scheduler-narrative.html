Chapter 03 · The forces

What pulls the plan apart

Hard constraints that don't bend

Every tank stays between its floor and ceiling, every day. Compartments respect product compatibility and last-cargo rules. Berths, pump rates and load-dependent draft against the tide. Clean, black and bitumen never mix.

Deep uncertainty

Cyclones and swell shut SPMs; ETAs slip; load ports congest; a Red Sea reroute adds two weeks around the Cape. A plan built on single-point estimates is optimistic by construction — and optimism is what breaks first.

Rolling, not one-shot

The month is a living plan. Because permits, nominations and downstream commitments pin to it, re-planning has real cost — so plan stability matters as much as optimality. Every replan solves the remainder, freezing what's committed.

A many-part objective

Freight and hire, speed-dependent bunkers, demurrage, port disbursements and tank-changeover — all traded against service. The cheapest plan and the most reliable plan are rarely the same one.

Chapter 04 · Whose problem it is

One plan, five different anxieties

Demand–supply planner · owns the monthly plan

Holds node inventories against a fixed fleet. A sudden demand spike or revised refinery run-plan ripples across every terminal — with no fast way to see which nodes now dry out, or whether the fleet still covers them.

Marine / chartering planner · sequences the voyages

Decides owned versus spot, sequences calls to dodge demurrage, absorbs off-hires and berth closures. The recurring question — “do I actually need a spot charter, and for which lift?” — is answered by intuition, not evidence.

Refinery / terminal ops · runs tanks & berths

Lives on ullage and evacuation. The classic failure is a vessel arriving when the tank can't take it — a berth free but no room to discharge — because a pipeline or rake slipped upstream of the ship.

Commercial / finance · owns the P&L

Carries charter economics and demurrage exposure. When a disruption forces a recovery, the question is blunt: what does this surge cost, and is there a cheaper way to stay whole?

Operations leadership · signs off the plan

Needs one glance to know the plan is feasible, resilient and priced — and, when something breaks, whether it's a shrug or a scramble. Not a spreadsheet forwarded three times.

Chapter 06 · How it works

From demand to a plan that defends itself

Build from where the fleet already is

The month is built from the opening position — where every vessel and tank sits on day one. The greedy constructor orders each demand by its earliest dry-out, assigns the cheapest hull that can reach it in time, and extends the voyage ballast → load → discharge → empty, honouring compartments, draft and berths. A GRASP multi-start perturbs the ordering and keeps the best feasible, lowest-cost plan — 100% served, in seconds.

Never “infeasible” — serve what the fleet can, charter the gap.

Score it, then close the gap

Every disruption is classified L0 (actualise only) → L4 (full replan) with its trigger reason and blast radius, so the response matches the severity. And the two-phase solve never just fails: it serves what the owned and time-chartered fleet can, then recommends a spot charter for the remainder — class, lane, quantity, by-day — or reports the exact shortfall, its earliest feasible date, and the binding constraint.

The same month, continued — never a blank restart.

Freeze what's committed, re-solve the rest

A replan is rolling. Everything executed or committed before the as-of day — legs underway, cargoes already loaded — is frozen; only the remainder is re-solved. The opening position is carried forward to that day, delays included, so a hull running five days late simply becomes available later and the plan is built around it. The output is the same July plan, continued — an immutable child version, diffed against its parent.

Project every tank, every day

Each node's stock is projected day by day: opening balance, plus net daily flow, plus each vessel operation on its day — a discharge adds, a load at the source subtracts. Dry-out is any day below the floor; tank-top any day above the ceiling; ullage is the headroom at a vessel's ETA — how much it can actually discharge.

stock(d) = opening + netDaily·d + Σ ops(≤d)
ullage(d) = smax − stock(d)
feasible ⇔ smin ≤ stock(d) ≤ smax ∀d

Chapter 08 · The mathematics

A Maritime Inventory Routing Problem, made tractable

Minimise cost, hold every constraint

min freight + bunker(speed) + demurrage + port + changeover
s.t. smin ≤ stockn,d ≤ smax  ∀ node, day
    compartment · draft · berth · compatibility

The inventory curve must stay inside the band at every node, every day — the constraint the whole plan is built to protect.

Heuristic-first, by necessity

A monolithic MILP over dozens of vessels, ~15 compartments each and a month of daily buckets is NP-hard and won't return in interactive time. A greedy builder — earliest dry-out first, cheapest available hull, ballast→load→discharge→empty — wrapped in a GRASP multi-start keeps the best feasible, lowest-cost plan. Watch it converge:

Optimisation you can trust

A compact LP relaxation (HiGHS) yields dual shadow prices — the marginal ₹/MT of each binding resource — surfaced as a bottleneck list. An independent validator then re-checks every hard constraint on the returned plan; optimiser and checker never share bookkeeping, so a breach can't hide.

Planning under uncertainty

Operational slack keeps a schedule from collapsing after the first leg. A Monte-Carlo replay against sampled, late-skewed delays fans the plan into thousands of futures and scores how it holds — reported as a resilience figure, stock-out exposure and the most-exposed nodes.
