Chapter 01 · The desk

Every tank on the coast, fed from a fixed fleet

A coastal planner at a national oil company carries a simple mandate and a hard one underneath it. Keep every terminal above its floor — no dry-out, no lost sales — while moving a million tonnes a month with the ships already on hire.

Today that runs on spreadsheets and phone calls. Inventory sits in one workbook, the fleet's noon-reports in an inbox, berth and draft limits in the planner's memory. It holds — until the sea disagrees.

Ten terminals. One fleet. Thirty-one days.

The July plan goes out — balanced on paper

Built from where every vessel and tank actually sits on day one.

The optimiser takes the month's movements and the opening position, orders demand by earliest dry-out, and assigns the cheapest hull that can reach each terminal in time. Ballast out, load, discharge, come back empty — a full month of voyages, feasible and priced, in seconds.

On paper it closes. Every node stays above its floor, the fleet is well used, demurrage is low. But this plan rests on a hundred quiet assumptions: on-time berthing, calm weather, demand landing exactly as forecast.

One of those assumptions is about to break.

Jul 12 · 06:40 · a depression forms over the Bay

Three things move at once

A cyclone on the Bay

Heavy weather off the east coast slows an Aframax carrying HSD toward Ennore. ETA slips three days.

⛔

Paradip SPM closes

The single-point mooring shuts for 4 days of swell. Any hull routed there now waits — or reroutes.

↑

Ennore lifts run hot

A downstream unit restarts early. HSD offtake at Ennore jumps sharply above plan for the week.

None of these is unusual. Together, in the same week, they unravel the plan.

Chapter 02 · The old way

Found late, fixed in a scramble

Jul 15 · a phone call

The dip at Ennore surfaces three days later, when the terminal calls to ask where its cargo is. Now the desk is behind the problem. Someone rebuilds half the month in a spreadsheet by hand; someone else works the phones for a ship.

A spot tanker is fixed under pressure, at a premium. A hull still routed to Paradip sits out the closure and racks up demurrage. The recovery holds — barely — but no one can say it was the cheapest way, or whether the next week is safe.

It knows the port is shut

The old way

Berth, draft and SPM status live in memory; the closure bites when a hull arrives.

The tower

Berth simultaneity, pump rate and load-dependent draft are hard constraints — and a date-range closure on the Paradip SPM flows straight into the replan, so nothing is routed into a shut berth.

Jul 12 · minutes later

It answered

A severity, not an alarm

The event is classified L2 — a contained disruption with a named blast radius: one node exposed, three voyages touched. That verdict tells the desk how hard to react: recover the remaining horizon, don't churn the whole month. The trigger, the thresholds it crossed, and the reason sit on the banner.

Multi-horizon: the month continues, it never restarts.

Freeze what's committed, re-solve the rest

Every leg underway and every cargo already loaded before Jul 12 is frozen. Only the remaining horizon is re-optimised, on the opening position carried forward to that day — the delayed Aframax simply becomes available later, the SPM stays shut through Jul 16. The result is the same July plan, continued as an immutable child version.

Never “infeasible” — the exact gap, and the exact fix.

One voyage charter, named and priced

The owned and time-chartered fleet covers most of the spike; the remainder becomes a single spot recommendation — one MR, HSD, Sikka to Ennore, load by Jul 15, with an estimated cost. Not a panicked fix at a premium, but the cheapest hull that closes the gap in time, decided while there is still time to fix it well.
